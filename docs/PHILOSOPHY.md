# The Philosophy of HMN

23/11/2026  ·  Working draft

We are building HMN (human) around a simple conviction: technology should help a person carry a coherent life forward, without making that person manage the machinery. Useful information should be available when it matters. Decisions should remain ours. The measure of progress is not how much we interact with a system, but how much attention it gives back.

This is a working foundation, not a claim that every capability exists or every question is settled. It describes the future we want to make more likely, and the commitments against which we should judge the steps we take.

## From applications to personal continuity

Our thesis is that many single-use applications, and the interfaces we navigate to operate them, will increasingly give way to a personal repository of data and information, with calm surfaces and agents above it. This is not a prediction that all apps disappear. Specialist tools will remain valuable. What should change is where continuity lives: with the person, rather than inside whichever application currently holds a fragment of their life.

Today, people often become the integration layer between their own tools. They copy details, reconstruct decisions and remember which system knows what. Each new service asks them to begin again. HMN should reduce that burden by preserving useful context across domains, without turning life into an endless data-entry exercise.

A collection of records is not enough. A decision has a history; a commitment involves other people; a note may matter because of the conversation that produced it. We want to preserve relationships and provenance, not flatten everything into disconnected facts. A system that can show where information came from is more useful, and more accountable, than one that merely sounds certain.

## A life system, not another destination

HMN is a complete personal life operating-system stack: the durable context, boundaries, intelligence and everyday surfaces that work together around a person. “Complete” describes the scope of responsibility, not a promise to build every tool or bring every activity into one interface. We should connect what helps, leave useful specialist tools intact and build incrementally rather than bet on one universal system winning.

An existing life ontology is one input into this thinking, not the product itself. HMN is neither a clone of a workspace tool nor a chat wrapper around an agent. A model can reason over context, but it should not become the only place that context exists. The intelligence must be replaceable; the personal context must endure.

That distinction changes what ownership means. Meaningful export, recovery and graceful offline use are aspirations we must make practical, not reassuring words in a settings screen. A person should be able to change a model, leave a vendor or replace a device without having to reconstruct their life from scratch.

## Calm is a purpose, not a visual style

The starting inspiration was “TRMNL for iOS”: glanceable, quiet information chosen by the person, rather than another engagement loop. The deeper idea is not a particular screen aesthetic. It is that a system can be useful without demanding to be visited, refreshed or fed.

Conversation belongs in HMN because speaking or writing is often the easiest way to express intent. It need not be the whole interface. A Today view can orient us; capture can hold something before it is lost; search can retrieve a source; an approval surface can make a decision clear. Each should fit its purpose rather than force every action through a transcript.

Work should disappear behind outcomes, not arrive as a flood of tool traces. That does not mean hiding consequential actions. It means distinguishing the information needed to decide from the detail needed to inspect. We should be able to see what happened and why, without being required to supervise every mechanical step.

## Assistance without surrender

Agents should extend human agency, not quietly absorb it. They can suggest, prepare and carry out bounded work. The owner must approve consequential changes. Permission to read something is not permission to act on it, and permission for one task is not an open-ended mandate.

This requires restraint as well as capability. An agent should have a clear identity and a limited scope of work, with access appropriate to that work. It should not acquire authority merely because it can reach another tool. Nor should activity become its own justification: selective responses to meaningful events are preferable to pointless polling and constant intervention.

There is a tension here. Asking for approval too often recreates the administrative burden we want to remove; asking too rarely removes meaningful control. We should resolve it through understandable boundaries and proportionate approvals, not through a promise of unbounded autonomy. When evidence is incomplete or intent is ambiguous, pausing can be the most helpful action. Human judgment remains necessary.

## Privacy is the foundation

A system that connects life domains can be unusually helpful and unusually intrusive. The same connection that makes a reminder useful can expose something never meant for that context. Privacy therefore cannot be a premium feature or a theatre of settings. It must determine what the system is allowed to do before intelligence is applied.

We distinguish access from security. Access asks who can see or use what; security asks how information is protected. Both matter. Our Public, Household, Personal, Private and Sealed boundaries must be enforced server-side, not entrusted to a prompt asking a model to behave. The detailed rules belong in the companion design principles; the philosophical point is that consent needs an enforceable boundary.

Data and models should be local-first. Private and Sealed information must remain local or on-device; cloud use should be optional and explicitly enabled only for eligible data. Local-first does not mean never using the internet. It means that participation in a remote service should not be the condition of possessing or using our own context.

The direction is a Mac mini personal server with an iPhone as a calm client. This makes the tradeoffs tangible: local ownership brings responsibility for availability, maintenance and recovery. Optional cloud services may offer convenience or capability, but neither convenience nor stronger models should silently expand permission. No arrangement removes every risk; our responsibility is to make the boundaries real and the remaining risks understandable.

## Trust must survive an ordinary bad day

Trust is earned through reliability, restraint, inspectability and recoverability. It is weakened when an ordinary failure appears catastrophic. If a personal server is offline, the client must not make that look like erased data or a lost relationship with the server. People need an honest account of what is unavailable, what remains safe and what they can do next.

The same honesty applies to memory and understanding. Stored information can be stale, incomplete or wrong. An inference is not a remembered fact; a suggestion is not a decision. HMN should make correction possible and preserve enough context to question an answer. Confidence should follow evidence, not the fluency of the model.

Reliability also includes a way back. We should aim for recoverable changes, useful records of important actions and exports that retain meaning outside HMN. These are commitments to work towards, not claims of completed implementation or perfect safety. A system deserves dependence only as it demonstrates that it can handle mistakes and interruptions.

## What a good day could feel like

Consider an illustrative day, not a description of anyone’s actual life. In the morning, Today shows a few chosen commitments and a preparation note linked to its source. A passing thought is captured quickly, without asking the person to choose a database or organise a hierarchy. Later, a relevant change prompts an agent to prepare an update within its permitted scope. A consequential change waits for approval, with the proposed effect clearly stated.

When the server becomes unavailable, the client explains that state and offers whatever remains safely available offline. It does not imply that the person’s history has vanished. When the connection returns, continuity resumes without demanding that the person reconstruct the interruption. These are experiences to build towards, not a claim that every part is already delivered.

The successful day is not one filled with impressive agent activity. It is one with fewer loose ends, less repeated explanation and more time spent on the life outside the system. We should judge HMN by attention returned, trusted continuity, useful context and reduced administration. If we create a new obligation to manage the assistant, we have missed the point. The future we want is not more software in the foreground. It is a person more able to act on what matters.
